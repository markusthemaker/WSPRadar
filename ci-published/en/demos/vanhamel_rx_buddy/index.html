Guided Demo

A 90° Antenna Rotation, a 3 dB Difference

See how antenna orientation changes reception.

Keep one antenna fixed, rotate the other, and follow the difference. This 160 m experiment shows how to find a change in WSPRadar, check it on one radio path, and see what the decoded signals add to the story.

Throughout this guided demo, ΔSNR compares signal quality when both setups report the same transmission, while ΔDecode compares how many transmissions each setup reported, including one-sided reception. A better SNR margin and more decodes are related benefits, but one does not automatically imply the other.

ON4AWM0 (JO20OT) / ON4AWM1 (JO20) · 160m · May 2021 · RX Benchmark

01

What were the operators testing?

Can turning a receiving antenna through 90° make a visible difference in WSPR results?

Jurgen Vanhamel, Walter Machiels and Hervé Lamy compared two receiving setups on 160 meters:

  • ON4AWM0 — Target: an ITA LWA II long-wire antenna, kept in the same orientation throughout the experiment.
  • ON4AWM1 — Reference: an adapted shorted dipole, initially parallel to the long wire and then rotated 90° in the horizontal plane after the first week.

In this RX Benchmark, Target and Reference are the two receiving setups. The stations plotted on the maps are the remote WSPR transmitters they heard. A station entry identifies a transmitter by its callsign and full reported locator. A reception report is a spot; repeated spots can strengthen the evidence without adding another station entry.

WSPRadar matches reports of the same transmitter and full reported locator, on the same band and in the same two-minute WSPR cycle. These spot pairs contain one retained report from each receiver and let us compare the setups at the same time. Multiple reports for one setup, station and cycle are consolidated using WSPRadar's SNR-selection rules before pairing. A Target-only observation means only ON4AWM0 reported that transmitter-cycle; Reference-only means only ON4AWM1 did.

Signal-to-noise ratio, or SNR, describes how strongly a signal stands out relative to noise. Higher SNR is better. Each pair supplies one ΔSNR, the Target SNR minus the corrected Reference SNR:

ΔSNR = ON4AWM0 SNR − (ON4AWM1 SNR + 1.6 dB)

Positive values favor the long-wire setup; negative values favor the dipole setup. The dipole is the antenna that changes; the long wire stays fixed.

The prepared correction accounts for an established receiving-chain offset: the receiver chains themselves can introduce a difference in reported SNR. Applying it helps avoid mistaking that offset for an antenna advantage. The companion One Antenna, Two Receivers — Why Calibration Matters explains that check. A constant correction shifts the SNR differences without changing the size of a before-and-after step.

Our question is: where, when and by how much did relative reception change—and did one setup also decode more transmissions?

03

Read the ΔSNR Map: does every direction agree?

Open Global View → ΔSNR Map. The map is centered on Target ON4AWM0. Compass sectors show direction from the Target, rings separate distance ranges, and their intersections form map segments. The markers show the remote transmitters.

Each colored segment shows the arithmetic mean—the average SNR difference for transmissions from the same transmitter decoded by both receivers in the same WSPR cycle. Every pair counts equally, so frequently heard transmitters have more influence on the average.

Look first at the paths within 2 500 km. The long wire leads toward ENE and WNW, while the dipole leads toward SSE: an overall advantage does not describe every direction. Select these regions in Segment Inspector and read the Spots mean and CI, which use the same pairs and weighting as the map.

The long-wire advantage is much larger toward ENE than WNW; toward SSE, the dipole has about a 4 dB advantage. All three regions meet the map's count and precision requirements. They describe the sampled paths across both orientations, not a radiation pattern or the change caused by rotation alone.

Hatching means the coloring requirements are not met—not that reception was absent. For example, SE has three qualifying stations but a CI half-width of about 0.85 dB, exceeding the 0.5 dB limit. Its observations remain in Inspector statistics. Blank segments have no observed station entries. Neutral colors cover a range around zero, not exact equality; read the numerical legend.

Station markers show reception categories, not which antenna had better SNR. Green Paired (qualifying) markers identify the 60 transmitters with pairs; none fall below this run's one-pair minimum. Only ON4AWM0 and Only ON4AWM1 identify stations reported exclusively by that receiver. A station with pairs can also supply one-sided reports in other cycles.

Move to Global View → Decodes & Stations by SNR Cutoff. Follow the curves from stronger signals on the left toward weaker signals on the right. These cumulative curves include reports at the displayed SNR cutoff or stronger; lowering the cutoff in 1 dB steps adds weaker reports. The upper row counts spots; the lower row counts distinct station entries. Each receiver's curves use its own raw reported SNR, without power normalization or Reference correction.

The Paired spots curves end at 5 379 observations each, representing the same transmissions from 60 stations. Their separation before the endpoint reflects different SNR values, not extra decodes. All these pairs also qualify for ΔSNR in the current run.

The upper-middle One-sided spots panel adds reception reported by only one receiver. Its weak-signal pattern is consistent with extra SNR margin helping reception; it does not establish a fixed decoding threshold or tell us why the other report is absent. The same transmitter can contribute pairs in some cycles and one-sided reports in others.

Read the right-hand endpoints as the observed totals:

The long wire recorded 1.29× as many decodes. The Exclusive stations panel adds a different lesson: 13 stations were reported only by the long wire, but two only by the dipole. More decodes and more stations are different benefits; repeated reception of a familiar transmitter increases the spot count without adding a station.

The annotation Both observed, no pairs: +0 stations means no station was heard by both receivers solely in different cycles. Either counts each observed transmitter-cycle or station once, giving 7 164 events and 75 station entries. A paired event contributes one decode to each receiver but only one event to Either. Do not add counts of stations with paired and one-sided reports as if they were separate sets.

In the lower row, each station enters a curve at its strongest relevant report. Its strongest paired report need not be its strongest report overall, so intermediate station-curve counts need not add even though the endpoints reconcile. The +1.6 dB correction changes neither these raw-SNR curves nor decode counts. Next, locate the additional reception on the map.

Read the ΔDecode Map: where were more signals decoded?

Open Global View → ΔDecode Map. ΔDecode compares the number of reported decodes: each receiver's total is Paired + its own one-sided decodes. WSPRadar pools those counts across the segment; it does not average station ratios.

Among these three directions, the largest SNR advantage does not produce the largest decoding advantage. ENE has much more SNR margin than WNW, yet almost equal decode totals. SSE provides the contrasting case where both measures favor the dipole.

In WNW at 0–2 500 km, the totals are 2 143 for ON4AWM0 and 1 866 for ON4AWM1:

2 143 ÷ 1 866 = 1.15× as many long-wire decodes, a net 277 additional decodes.

Compare the same three directions with the SNR map:

Extra SNR can provide margin without many additional decodes. ENE illustrates that distinction; WNW has a smaller SNR advantage but a larger decoding advantage. ΔSNR averages paired differences, whereas ΔDecode includes all retained paired and one-sided reports. Read them together rather than expecting their rankings to match.

On the legend, 2× means twice as many decodes for the indicated setup. Warm colors favor Target; blue colors favor Reference. The central light-green band extends to the 1.1× boundaries in either direction, so it does not necessarily mean a tie.

Coloring requires one observed station and 72 observed events per segment: 1% of the run's 7 164 events, rounded up, with each paired event counted once. These requirements include one-sided stations and are independent of ΔSNR qualification. Hatching means insufficient support for coloring, not equal reception; the observations remain available to inspect.

Interpret count differences under comparable operation and reporting: an outage or missing upload can also remove a report. Transmissions missed by both remain unknown, so this is not an absolute decoding probability. The next section brings the directions together and checks how the overall result depends on weighting.

06

Read Benchmark Statistics: how large is the overall difference?

Open Segment Inspector → Benchmark Statistics with Full Range | All Directions selected.

Both weighting approaches favor the long wire, but giving each transmitter equal weight produces a smaller, less precise average advantage. The upper-left ΔSNR ± 95% CI - Qualifying spot pairs panel weights each pair equally; the upper-right ΔSNR ± 95% CI - Qualifying station means shows the mean of station means: it first averages each station's pairs, then gives each station mean equal weight. Their results match the Spots and Stations cards.

We use Student-t confidence intervals as a practical summary of the mean’s estimated precision: the calculation accounts for both the number of observations and their variability. The ± value describes uncertainty in the mean, not the spread of individual reports. Here, both weighting approaches support the same clear advantage across the full-period sample.

For example, +2.14 ± 0.08 dB represents a 95% mean interval of approximately +2.05 to +2.22 dB, calculated before rounding. It does not mean that most individual reports lie within that narrow range.

Summary Mean ΔSNR ± 95% CI Equal weight for each…
All qualifying spot pairs +2.14 ± 0.08 dB one of 5 379 pairs
Mean of station means +1.29 ± 0.77 dB one of 60 station means

The histograms group similar results into bars: the horizontal axis shows the difference, and bar height shows the share of evidence in that range. The distributions also extend below zero, showing comparisons that favor the dipole. Frequently heard transmitters influence the pooled result more. The station-weighted result includes eight stations with only one pair and two with two pairs: equal weight does not mean equally much supporting evidence. Check a station's pair count alongside its mean in Station Insights.

As a secondary check, the median is the middle value after sorting. The paired median is +2.4 dB; the lower-right ΔSNR - Qualifying station medians panel gives +1.4 dB for the median of the 60 station medians. Both also favor the long wire, but they are not means, and their difference is not an uncertainty interval.

The lower-left ΔDecode - Spot Decodes panel groups stations by decode advantage and weights its bars by receiver decodes, not equally by station. Its 12 543 decodes count each paired event twice, once per receiver; Either counts that event once. The large bar around 1× shows that much reception came from paths with similar totals. Additional reports toward Target move the pooled result to 1.29×. The Exclusive endpoints contain decodes from exclusively heard stations, not every one-sided report.

All 60 paired stations contribute here, including those in hatched map segments. Neither full-period mean measures the rotation change: both combine the two orientations. To find the change, follow the observations through time.

08

Read ΔDecode over Time: check the counts behind the pattern

Open Segment Inspector → Temporal Evidence → ΔDecode over Time, keeping Full Range | All Directions, 12h and UTC.

Repeated overnight reception supports the broad SNR pattern; daytime values can rest on very few observations. This view helps distinguish a recurring advantage from a striking value in a sparsely sampled interval.

The upper row counts distinct stations within each time bin. Both observed means each receiver heard that station at least once in the bin; those receptions need not be in the same cycle. The lower row counts transmitter-cycle events, where Paired requires the same cycle. In the supplied figure, green represents these shared categories, magenta Target-only and cyan Reference-only. Repeated decodes of an already-heard transmitter can enlarge the lower bars without increasing the upper station count.

Read the overlaid lines against the right-hand axes: 1× means equal totals; above favors Target; below favors Reference. The upper line compares distinct-station totals, the lower line total decodes. The right-hand panels show average counts by UTC hour; their advantage is calculated from the combined counts, not by averaging daily ratios.

For example, the all-station SNR point at 13:00–14:00 UTC is +7.4 dB—but rests on just one spot pair across the whole run, with no calculable mean CI. The one-pair station minimum retains this observation; it does not guarantee well-populated time bins.

Check the count behind a striking value. A large ratio on a tiny bar and a repeated advantage across hundreds of comparisons carry different amounts of evidence. Now follow a well-observed transmitter to test whether the upward SNR change remains on the same path.

The refreshed figures on this page show means and 95% CIs. The exact before/after means above and their +3.73 ± 0.14 dB change are reconstruction results, not numbers printed in the paper.

The split after 750 spot pairs follows the publication's observation boundary as applied to the reconstruction. It is not an independently logged UTC rotation time. WSPRadar adds maps, decoded counts and individual reports around that comparison; these are not claims that the paper published the same decoding analysis.

Calibration note: This demo uses +1.6 dB, rounded from 1.57 dB documented on the authors' website. The paper reports +1.2 dB, as examined in the companion calibration demo. Using +1.2 dB here would move every paired difference 0.4 dB higher, but leave the 3.73 dB mean change unchanged. Agreement with the reconstructed figure does not establish which correction the authors used to draw it.

↳

Then check M7AEO's decodes

Open its ΔDecode over Time chart, retaining 12h and UTC.

The large SNR transition does not mean a similarly large difference in decoded counts. Across the run, M7AEO supplies 1 550 long-wire decodes versus 1 448 dipole decodes, or 1.07×. These comprise 1 441 paired events, 109 Target-only and seven Reference-only observations. The large overnight bars contain mostly paired reception: both receivers often reported the same transmissions even while their relative SNR changed.

For a concrete contrast, 00:00–01:00 UTC, pooled across dates, has a mean paired difference of +1.04 ± 0.40 dB from 138 pairs, with 138 decodes at each receiver: 1×. Here the long wire supplied extra SNR margin without additional observed decodes.

Check sparse intervals too: 9 May, 05:15–17:15 UTC has a +4.4 dB mean from one spot pair, with no calculable mean CI. The broad transition rests on the repeated observations, not that isolated high point.

The 1.07× total combines both orientations; it does not measure how much the rotation itself changed decoding. To check what an individual SNR difference represents, continue to Drill-Down.

With M7AEO selected, open Drill-Down. The retained observation rows give these four examples of how the comparison is calculated:

The individual reports make the sign and correction concrete. The first comparison favors the dipole. On 8 May, −16.0 − (−20.4) = +4.4 dB, favoring the long wire. These processed SNR values use a common 1 W (30 dBm) reported-power basis. The Reference column already includes the +1.6 dB correction; do not add it again. The earlier cumulative curves use raw reported SNR. The common power adjustment on both sides leaves ΔSNR unchanged.

Use Zoom window to focus the plots and table; Filter table changes only the displayed rows. This view is for checking arithmetic or investigating a surprising point. The before-and-after result comes from the full observation groups, not from choosing two individual rows. Preserve those observations with the settings so the comparison can be revisited.

11

Download & Share Results: take the method to your own station

You have followed a physical antenna change down to individual WSPR reports—and learned to distinguish a better SNR margin, additional decoded transmissions and additional stations heard.

On M7AEO's path, 1 441 same-cycle comparisons show a 3.73 ± 0.14 dB change in mean ΔSNR across the reconstructed split, favoring the fixed long wire relative to the rotated dipole. Across both orientations and all sampled paths, the long wire leads by +2.14 ± 0.08 dB across pairs and records 1.29× as many decodes. The maps also show paths where the dipole leads.

These are results for the installed receiving setups, sampled paths and experiment period—not a universal antenna-gain change or an identical effect in every direction.

For your own antenna experiment, use the same sequence: find the difference on the maps, follow it through time, check a well-observed transmitter, then inspect the underlying reports. Keep notes on antenna orientation, the time of each change, receiver settings and common operating periods. If a sector or time interval remains sparsely sampled, collect more comparable nights before drawing a strong conclusion.

Under Download & Share Results, select Prepare All Results for Download, then Download Prepared Results, to preserve the configuration, figures and processed observations in the results package. Save Config separately preserves reusable settings; Share Analysis encodes them in a URL that the recipient opens and runs. Keep station notes alongside the package so a later comparison records what was physically connected, when both setups were operating and what changed.
