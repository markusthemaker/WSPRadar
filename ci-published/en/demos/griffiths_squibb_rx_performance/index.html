A Month on 40 m with a Low Dipole

What did this receiver hear—and how consistently?

A distant WSPR spot tells you that a path worked once. A month of reports lets you ask a more useful station question: where did reception work regularly, where was it occasional, and when did it change? This guided demo follows G3ZIL on 40 m during April 2017, from its worldwide reception map to individual observations of one transmitter.

RX Performance examines one receiving station, without a Reference receiver. We will read station reach, decoding frequency and successful SNR together. G3ZIL heard transmitters almost 19 200 km away, but its more dependable reception was much closer to home. Even one well-observed European path behaved very differently at different hours.

For a comparison between two receiving setups, see Low Dipole vs Radial-Free Vertical. That RX Benchmark compares G3ZIL with G4HZX; this guide asks what G3ZIL achieved on its own.

01

The station and the question

Gwyn Griffiths, G3ZIL, and Nigel Squibb, G4HZX, described their investigations in Improving HF Band SNR, published in Practical Wireless in October 2017. G3ZIL used a homebuilt direct-conversion receiver in a garden shed and a low dipole, about 4 m high at its centre and 1.5 m at the ends. Their account records retuning the dipole on 8 April. The installation was a working station, not an unchanged laboratory fixture. Original paper

Our practical question is: what did this complete receiving setup hear, across which paths and at which times? The paper supplies the station context. RX Performance examines this one receiver without a Reference. The single-station findings below are WSPRadar's analysis of the historical reports, not results claimed by the paper or a measurement of the dipole's gain.

The Target is the receiving station under examination: G3ZIL at IO90HW. The remote stations are transmitters. A station identity is an exact callsign and its full reported locator. The same callsign at different locators remains separate; an identity count is therefore not necessarily a count of different operators or physical transmitters.

What counts as a reception opportunity?

A spot is an uploaded report of a successful WSPR decode. Reports show what was received, not every transmission or every period a receiver was listening. WSPRadar needs supporting activity evidence before it can count an absent report as a miss.

A confirmed opportunity is one transmission for which the reports establish that the transmitter was on air and G3ZIL was receiving on the same band in the same WSPR cycle, a two-minute interval. It answers “was there an observed transmission during confirmed receiving activity?”, not “should this path necessarily have been decodable?” A successful G3ZIL report establishes both ends by itself. Without a G3ZIL report of that transmitter, two separate pieces of evidence are needed:

Evidence in that cycle What counts
G3ZIL reports the transmitter. A success. The decode itself establishes the opportunity, even if no other receiver reported the transmission.
Reports from other receivers establish that the transmitter was active. G3ZIL reports a different transmitter on the same band and cycle, but not this one. A confirmed miss. There is evidence of the transmission and of G3ZIL's receiving activity.
The necessary activity evidence is absent. An unknown outcome, excluded rather than counted as a miss.

For example, suppose reports from other receivers establish that HB0QRP was transmitting at 18:20 and G3ZIL reports HB9TSO in that same cycle. HB0QRP was transmitting and G3ZIL was receiving, so an absent G3ZIL report of HB0QRP counts as a confirmed miss. If G3ZIL reports nothing in that cycle, the reports about HB0QRP alone cannot establish an opportunity for G3ZIL. The Drill-Down example below returns to this distinction in actual reports.

Each exact transmitter identity contributes at most one opportunity per cycle. Several receivers reporting the same transmitter still establish only one opportunity for G3ZIL. External confirmation does not count a successful Target decode twice.

Decode Rate is 100 × successful decodes / confirmed opportunities. Its denominator includes successes and confirmed misses. A cycle with no G3ZIL report cannot establish its receiving activity and does not become a row of assumed failures.

This is reception during observable activity, not an uptime test. Another receiver hearing a transmitter also does not establish that its path to G3ZIL should have worked. A geographically varied beacon population is not a test for which every receiver should score 100%.

Which stations are included?

An eligible station is a transmitter identity retained by the run's filters that meets the selected Minimum confirmed opportunities per station. The map control calls these qualifying stations; in this Performance guide both expressions refer to the same included station population. We use eligible stations in the explanations, with each station still counted by its exact callsign and full reported locator.

Eligibility does not require a successful G3ZIL decode. A station heard only elsewhere can qualify through confirmed misses and contribute a 0% station rate. This keeps the analysis from describing only the paths G3ZIL managed to hear. The next section explains why this exploratory demo retains stations with as little as one confirmed opportunity but requires more stations before a map segment receives a color.

Two evidence settings serve different purposes. Minimum confirmed opportunities per station is 1. We deliberately use the lowest minimum to retain sparsely observed paths and occasional reception: maximum sensitivity to evidence of a path, not a change in the receiver's RF sensitivity. A retained transmitter with one opportunity has a rate of either 0% or 100%. That describes its one observation, not dependable reception over the month.

Minimum qualifying stations per map segment is 5. A direction–distance segment therefore needs at least five eligible stations before its average receives a color; one station can no longer stand in for an entire region. Segments with one to four eligible stations remain visible with hatching, and their stations remain in the cards, Inspector and station evidence.

The two requirements complement each other: one preserves rare observations; five demands broader station support for map coloring. Five stations do not make a sparsely observed station's rate precise or guarantee independent paths, statistical confidence or a robust regional estimate. Read opportunity counts as well as station counts. Raising only the map minimum changes shading, not the eligible population or the whole-run At a glance values.

A rare long-distance reception and a much nearer typical successful report can coexist.

Read Global View: a DX footprint is not the same as regular reception

Open Global View. G3ZIL sits at the centre; compass sectors show direction from its QTH and rings show distance. Reception was much more consistent on nearby ESE paths than on the more distant WNW paths—even though the distant sector contributed more heard transmitters.

A map segment combines a direction and distance range. Its Station-averaged Decode Rate is the arithmetic mean of the eligible stations' individual rates: add their percentages, then divide by the number of stations. Every identity counts equally, including identities never heard. More reports from a station do not give its rate extra weight here.

Markers answer a different question. Heard by Target means at least one successful decode; Heard by others only means no successful Target decode during that station's confirmed opportunities. A green marker can still represent a path with many misses. Read the numerical color legend rather than interpreting marker density as reception consistency.

04

Read Spot & Station Decodes: many paths, very different levels of consistency

In Segment Inspector, keep Full Range | All Directions and Summary statistic: Mean. G3ZIL heard 1 102 eligible stations, while more than half of the eligible stations were never decoded. That is not a failure to reach an expected 100% score: the worldwide population includes observed transmissions on many paths that need not have supported reception at G3ZIL.

Eligible stations are not every transmitter in the world or every possible global transmission. They are the stations for which this run retained confirmed opportunities on the selected band during observable G3ZIL activity. 100% is the mathematical upper bound for that retained sample, not a practical worldwide reception target or a calibrated measure of how much hardware improvement remains. Receiving every confirmed transmission from all those paths is not an expectation for this station.

These percentages are useful baselines for your own later Performance runs—for example after an antenna or receiver change—when band, geography, thresholds, duration and operating periods are comparable. Check the contributing station population and propagation context too. A change in the percentage can reflect changed paths or conditions as well as your station; it does not by itself measure the benefit of one component. The cards and six-panel figure show how to investigate that distinction.

Read the two cards together

Spots counts 114 228 successful decodes in 673 887 confirmed opportunities, giving a Spot Decode Rate of 17.0%. Pooled opportunities means adding the opportunities of all eligible stations into one combined count, then dividing their combined successes by that total. Each station–cycle opportunity counts once; this is not a new category of evidence. A transmitter observed in many cycles therefore has more influence than one observed only a few times.

Stations counts 1 102 heard out of 2 444 eligible stations, giving 45.1% Station Reach. Its green Station-averaged Decode Rate is 19.4%, using one equally weighted rate per identity, as on the map. These are three different questions: how many opportunities succeeded, how many stations were ever heard, and how often the individual station paths succeeded on average.

The current distance rows make the geographic contrast more precise:

On the 5 000–10 000 km paths, 173 eligible stations were heard, but the 2.2% Spot Decode Rate uses the combined opportunities of all 981 eligible stations in that distance range, including the stations never heard. Pooling here means adding 4 995 successful decodes and 223 367 confirmed misses, giving 228 362 opportunities, then calculating the successful fraction. It is not a rate calculated only from the 173 heard stations. The much nearer paths were both more widely reached and more frequently decoded. Small differences between the two distant reach percentages should not obscure that large contrast.

Why do the two Decode Rates differ?

The two rates describe the same evidence but ask different questions. Consider this illustrative pair of stations, not another result from G3ZIL:

Eligible station Successful decodes Confirmed opportunities Individual Decode Rate
A 90 100 90%
B 5 10 50%

Station-averaged Decode Rate asks: how often did each station's path work, giving each station equal influence? Calculate each rate first, then average them: (90% + 50%) / 2 = 70%. A and B each contribute half of the result, despite their unequal opportunity counts.

Spot Decode Rate asks: what fraction of all observed opportunities succeeded? Add the counts before dividing: (90 + 5) / (100 + 10) = 95 / 110 = 86.4%. Station A supplied 100 of the 110 opportunities, so it has ten times the influence of B in this pooled rate. Its higher success rate pulls the combined result nearer 90%.

Neither calculation is the correct replacement for the other. One describes reception across equally weighted stations; the other describes reception across equally weighted opportunities. In G3ZIL's full-scope result, the relationship is reversed: the 17.0% pooled rate is below the 19.4% equal-station mean, so opportunity-count weighting pulls the summary downward. Station Reach is different again: in the illustrative example both stations were heard, so reach is 100%, despite rates of 70% and 86.4%.

All distances combines the underlying counts or individual station rates, not the displayed percentages of the distance rows. Scope matters too: the paths beyond 10 000 km add 30 heard stations, but just 212 successes in 36 747 opportunities. Adding those paths changes the overall percentages without changing the reception already observed nearer G3ZIL. Keep geography and thresholds comparable when comparing runs.

Read the SNR-band figure: successful reports and distinct stations are not interchangeable

Keep Full Range | All Directions and Summary statistic: Mean. A small share of weak successful reports can involve many different stations. The figure separates the volume of reception from the number of paths reached, then shows how regularly those paths worked.

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) describes signal level relative to noise. Here it is power-normalized: adjusted using reported transmit power to a common 1 W (30 dBm) basis. Read the five SNR panels from weaker to stronger in 5 dB bands. The adjusted values can extend beyond the range expected for raw WSPR reports; normalization changes their plotted SNR, not which transmissions were successfully decoded.

Where do most successful reports fall?

Read Successful Spot Decodes and Spot Decode Rate together. The largest band, −15 to below −10 dB, contains 22 399 decodes. Those same decodes contribute 3.3 percentage points to the overall 17.0% Spot Decode Rate, because the lower panel divides each band’s count by all confirmed opportunities, including misses.

The upper panel uses a logarithmic count scale; the lower shows contributions to the overall success percentage. Both red markers show Mean spot SNR: −11.9 dB, with every successful report contributing once. These are two ways of reading the same successful reports.

Do weak reports represent many different stations?

Read Stations with Successful Decodes and Station Reach together. Only 3.4% of successful reports fall below −30 dB on this normalized scale, yet they include 412 of the 1 102 stations heard. A relatively small number of weak reports therefore represents a substantial part of the observed station reach. Many of those stations were also heard at stronger SNR.

The solid portion assigns each station to its weakest successful SNR band. Hatching identifies stations also heard in a weaker band—not repeated decodes within that band. A station can appear in several bars, so do not add the complete bars together. Station Reach expresses these same counts as percentages of all eligible stations.

The red marker, Mean weakest station SNR: −25.4 dB, averages one lowest successful value per heard station. It describes the weak end of those recorded paths, not a receiver sensitivity threshold.

How regularly were the stations heard?

Station Decode Frequency changes the horizontal axis from SNR to each station’s overall Decode Rate. Here 54.9% of eligible stations were never decoded, while 16.7% were decoded in more than half their confirmed opportunities. The bar height is the share of stations in a group, not their decoding-success percentage.

This distribution shows why the overall mean does not describe every path. Support still matters: with the selected minimum of one opportunity, a single success can give a station a 100% rate without establishing regular reception.

How does this connect back to the map?

Station-averaged Decode Rate gives every eligible station equal weight. For each SNR band, divide each station’s successes in that band by all its confirmed opportunities, then average those percentages across all eligible stations—including zeros.

Here the −15 to below −10 dB band contributes 2.9 percentage points to the 19.4% station mean, compared with its 3.3-point contribution to Spot Decode Rate. The difference is weighting, not different decodes.

The All SNR bar uses each station’s complete rate. This is the map’s calculation for the same station population, not an average of map colors. Its red marker, Mean of station mean SNR: −17.4 dB, is a separate signal-level summary: each heard station’s mean successful SNR counts equally.

Mean or Median? The median is the middle ordered value, averaging the two middle values for an even count. Selecting Median changes the station-rate summaries and SNR markers, but not the counts, Spot Decode Rate, Station Reach or frequency groups. The overall station median is 0% here because more than half the eligible stations were never heard. The map retains its arithmetic mean. Restore Mean before continuing.

The separate All SNR bars reconnect the panels to the cards above. Missed signals have no measured Target SNR, so these plots describe successful reception—not the probability of decoding a signal at a given SNR. Next, Temporal Evidence shows when that reception occurred.

05

Read Temporal Evidence: when did successful signal levels change?

Keep Full Range | All Directions, 12h, Time basis: UTC and SNR statistic: Mean. Begin with G3ZIL (RX) Successful SNR over Time, the first visible figure in Temporal Evidence. The successful reports are weaker in the early UTC hours and stronger toward the evening; the month does not sit at one fixed SNR level.

Over Time (12 h bins) follows April in date order. By UTC Hour (1 h bins) combines matching hours across the month: all observations from 19:00 to before 20:00, for example. It is a folded hourly view, not the continuation of the chronological axis or a record of one typical day. Dates with more successful observations contribute more values; the calculation does not first average the days or the stations.

The vertical axis shows successful normalized SNR, with no station baseline subtracted. The background Relative spot density (% of panel maximum) shows where observations concentrate. Each panel has its own maximum: 100 marks its most populated cell, not 100% reception or 100% of its reports. The line is Bin mean; the accompanying Bin 95% CI describes the estimated mean in each bin.

We use Student-t confidence intervals as a practical summary of the mean’s estimated precision: the calculation accounts for both the number of observations and their variability. The ± value describes uncertainty in the mean, not the spread of individual reports. These are pointwise intervals—one interval for each bin—and the calculation assumes independent observations. Consecutive WSPR reports need not be independent, so the nominal intervals are not a guarantee of precision in another run.

The selected HB0QRP example below will make the distinction concrete: −13.4 ± 0.5 dB means bounds of about −13.9 to −12.9 dB for that hourly mean, not a claim that individual reports occupied that narrow range. If you select Median, the accompanying Q1–Q3 band spans the middle 50% of observations; its width is the interquartile range (IQR). It describes spread, not uncertainty of the mean. Keep Mean for this guide.

Changing Time aggregation to 6h or 1h gives more chronological detail without changing the one-hour folded bins. Local Solar instead uses solar time at G3ZIL's QTH, with solar noon at 12:00; it is not local civil time or the clock at every transmitter. Keep UTC when checking the dated examples below.

Higher successful SNR alone does not establish more successful reception. Weak signals can disappear from the decoded sample, and a changing mixture of transmitters can also move the mean. The next figures supply the opportunities and distances needed to interpret that SNR pattern.

Read the reception history alongside the SNR pattern

In Temporal Evidence, continue to G3ZIL (RX) Decode Rate over Time with the same scope, 12h bins and UTC. Reception across this population was much more frequent in the early evening than before dawn. These are pooled rates across the observed transmitters, not an equal-station time series.

Add distance: which paths supply the pattern?

Decode Rate by Distance over Time shows that the higher rates are concentrated in nearer distance bands, while distant reception remains sparse. The hourly panel helps locate those differences within the day; the chronological panel shows whether they persist or appear only briefly.

Each cell divides its successes by its confirmed opportunities. The two panels share a numerical scale whose maximum is 50% in this supplied figure. Its strongest color therefore means 50%, not 100%. The scale can change with the selection, so compare legend values between figures. A cell with opportunities but no success is a valid 0%; an empty cell has no rate. An isolated colored cell needs its opportunity count checked before it becomes a reception claim.

Optional Expert Review: compare each path with its own usual level

Expand Expert Review — SNR deviation from each station’s own run mean. The weakening around 20 April also appears after each transmitter's own usual successful SNR has been removed. This adds a different perspective from the absolute-SNR figure; it still does not identify the cause.

A station baseline is that transmitter's mean successful normalized SNR over the complete run. It requires at least three successful observations with usable SNR; 831 identities meet this separate requirement. A SNR deviation is one successful observation minus its station baseline. A report of −10 dB against a −15 dB baseline gives +5 dB. The 0 dB line means the path's own baseline, not zero signal or equality between receivers.

The current figure pools individual deviations, giving every successful observation one contribution. It does not give each station one vote per bin. On 19 April, 12:00–24:00 UTC, the mean deviation is +1.5 dB; on 20 April it is −2.9 dB in 00:00–12:00 and −3.1 dB in 12:00–24:00. The hourly profile similarly runs below baseline around late morning and midday, then above it in the evening.

NOAA's report for 20 April records moderate G2 geomagnetic storm conditions. That is relevant external context, not evidence that the storm caused this reception change. The station baselines also come from the same run, and the interval calculation holds them fixed rather than adding uncertainty from their estimation.

The geographic and temporal views now point to useful questions. Following one well-observed transmitter removes changes in transmitter identity from the comparison, while leaving the actual conditions along that path to be investigated.

Open Station Insights: choose a path with something to teach

Open Station Insights and leave Filter by selected segment and distance off to browse the full analysis. HB0QRP is useful because it supplies thousands of opportunities and a clear hourly contrast—not because it represents every European path.

The saved list contains the 1 102 eligible stations heard by G3ZIL. Enable Heard only by other stations. to also reveal the 1 342 eligible stations with no successful Target decodes, giving 2 444 in total. Those zero-success identities already contributed to the map and rate denominators; the switch changes visibility, not the scientific result.

Heard by Target counts successes; Heard by others only counts confirmed misses. Their sum is the station's opportunity count. Decode Rate (%) divides successes by that sum. Median SNR @ 30 dBm describes successful normalized reports only. In the CSV these counts appear as Target (T) and Elsewhere (E); they have the same meanings.

The optional geographic filter narrows the list to the Inspector selection. A table Filter, sorting, or row selection does not recalculate the map or Inspector. Select HB0QRP (JN47), approximately 844 km, 113° ESE. G3ZIL decoded it 3 412 times in 7 762 confirmed opportunities, giving 44.0% Decode Rate. Its median successful normalized SNR is −15.0 dB. That monthly summary is neither continuous reception nor its performance at every hour. Selected Station Evidence shows what it conceals.

Check HB0QRP's Decode Rate: stronger successful reports do not always mean more decodes

In the selected station's Decode Rate over Time, keep 12h and UTC. HB0QRP has a morning opening and an afternoon/evening opening, separated by much less frequent late-morning reception. Its strongest successful-SNR hour is not automatically its highest Decode Rate.

The late-morning reduction has hundreds of confirmed opportunities behind it and accompanies weaker successful SNR. The evening reports have higher mean SNR than the morning reports, yet the evening Decode Rate is slightly lower. Successful signal quality and decoding frequency are complementary observations, not interchangeable scores. These are retained hourly summaries, not a statistical test that one operating hour will outperform another in a future month.

What this run teaches—and how to use it at your station

G3ZIL's low-dipole setup achieved broad but uneven reception during April 2017: 1 102 eligible stations heard, including occasional reception at 19 194 km, with substantially more consistent results on nearby paths than on distant ones. Across the retained worldwide population, 45.1% Station Reach, 17.0% Spot Decode Rate and 19.4% Station-averaged Decode Rate describe different aspects of that outcome.

The six-panel SNR view shows why repeated decodes and distinct stations must be counted separately. The time views locate changing reception by hour, date and distance. HB0QRP then makes the pattern tangible on one path: frequent morning and evening reception, a late-morning reduction, and a substantially weaker half-day on 22 April than on 16 April. Drill-Down connects those summaries to actual station-cycle observations.

For your own station, start with a documented receiving setup and a completed period. Use geography to choose a question, read reach and rates with their counts, then follow a well-supported station through time. Keep scope and evidence settings comparable between runs. A new period under suitably controlled, comparable conditions tests experimental repeatability; different views of this April record do not constitute separate experiments.

The original paper investigated installed stations through comparisons and operating changes. This Performance analysis adds a baseline for one receiver. It does not reconstruct the paper's comparative findings or isolate antenna gain. A controlled RX Benchmark is the appropriate next step for a two-setup question with known common operation.
