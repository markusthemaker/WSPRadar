Low Dipole vs Radial-Free Vertical

A low dipole versus a vertical on 40 m — a real-world comparison using WSPR data.

Does better signal-to-noise ratio also mean more signals decoded? This guided demo follows two receiving installations through geography, time and an individual transmitter. ΔSNR compares signal quality when both receivers report the same transmission; ΔDecode compares all observed decodes, including reception reported by only one receiver.

G3ZIL (IO90HW) / G4HZX (IO91) · 40m · April 2017 · RX Benchmark

Original paper ↗

01

The comparison

Two installations. The same transmitted signals.

Gwyn Griffiths, G3ZIL, and Nigel Squibb, G4HZX, wanted to understand and improve reception on 40 m, including the effects of antennas and local noise. They used essentially the same homebuilt receiver design at different sites.

G3ZIL — Target: a low horizontal dipole, about 4 m at its centre and 1.5 m at its ends. The receiver was moved to a garden shed, with improvements to grounding and power-supply noise suppression.

G4HZX — Reference: a Hustler 5-BTV vertical, connected to a ground spike without radials, at the far end of the garden.

This analysis covers April 2017 on 40 m, within 10 000 km. WSPRadar pairs reports from the same transmitter, identified by callsign and full reported locator, on the same band, in the same two-minute cycle. Each spot pair matches one retained Target observation and one retained Reference observation. The comparison concerns complete receiving installations: antennas, sites and local noise all contribute.

02

Paired signal quality

A large overall SNR advantage

Here, ΔSNR is G3ZIL SNR minus G4HZX SNR. Positive ΔSNR means higher reported SNR at G3ZIL.

Across 57 767 spot pairs: +6.55 ± 0.05 dB.

Across 792 equally weighted station means: +6.40 ± 0.41 dB.

The paired-observation mean gives every spot pair equal weight. The mean of station means first averages each transmitter’s paired differences, then gives every station equal weight.

The advantage remains about 6–7 dB when every transmitting station has equal influence. Both averages are strongly positive, with narrow nominal 95% confidence intervals.

We use Student-t confidence intervals as a practical summary of the mean’s estimated precision: the calculation accounts for both the number of observations and their variability. The ± value describes uncertainty in the mean, not the spread of individual reports. Here, both weighting approaches support the same clear advantage. Individual SNR differences vary much more.

Comparing both maps

Geographic patterns

The ΔSNR map shows where that overall signal-quality result changes. Nearby eastern paths, 0–2 500 km, average +6.82 dB. Distant west-northwest paths, 5 000–10 000 km, average only +0.08 dB, close to parity.

The ΔDecode map shows whether those paths also contributed more reported decodes. G3ZIL recorded 1.67× as many decodes on nearby eastern paths, versus 1.02× in distant WNW.

Both maps show broadly similar geographic patterns: a larger paired-SNR advantage generally accompanies more decoded signals. The agreement is not universal, as the nearby NE sector illustrates.

SNR over time

The advantage changed through April—and through the day

Chronological view: how did the comparison develop through April?

G3ZIL’s paired-SNR advantage increased during the first half of the month, recovering the broad trend in the paper’s Figure 3. For the 12–24 UTC intervals, the mean rose from about +1.6 dB on 1 April to +8.7 dB on 15 April, with fluctuations along the way.

Folded by UTC hour: how did the comparison vary through the day?

This panel combines observations from the same UTC hour across April. For example, it groups all observations from 12:00–13:00, regardless of date. G3ZIL’s advantage was generally smaller overnight and larger during the day.

This recurring pattern is consistent with changing propagation and the mix of paths being received. It helps show when the installations perform differently, without identifying a particular propagation mode.

The paper investigated decreasing soil moisture as a possible explanation for the early-April trend. The WSPRadar time view alone does not establish its cause.

01

Setup: what did the operators compare?

In Improving HF Band SNR (Practical Wireless, October 2017), Gwyn Griffiths, G3ZIL, and Nigel Squibb, G4HZX, compared reception on 40 m using essentially the same homebuilt direct-conversion receiver design at different locations. Original paper

Target G3ZIL — IO90HW, the receiving station being evaluated: a horizontal dipole about 4 m high at its center and 1.5 m at its ends, with the receiver moved to a garden shed. The paper records retuning the dipole on 8 April.

Reference G4HZX — IO91, the receiving station used for comparison: a Hustler 5-BTV vertical connected to a ground spike, without radials, at the far end of the garden.

How did these complete receiving installations compare during April 2017? Antennas, sites, local noise and any operating changes all belong to this comparison. We are not isolating antenna design from everything else that affects reception.

A spot is an uploaded reception report following a successful WSPR decode. A station identity identifies a remote transmitter by its callsign and full reported locator.

WSPRadar forms spot pairs when both receivers report that same transmitter on the same band in the same two-minute WSPR cycle. This compares reception at the same time, rather than one receiver’s good afternoon with the other’s reception on a different day.

One-sided spots are reports without a matching report from the other receiver in that cycle. Target-only means G3ZIL reported the transmission and G4HZX did not; Reference-only means the reverse. They provide reception evidence, but no second measured SNR to subtract. Reports alone cannot distinguish a missed signal from an operating or reporting gap, and transmissions missed by both remain unknown.

The fixed scope is 40 m, all directions, 0–10 000 km, from 1 April 2017 00:00 UTC to 1 May 00:00 UTC, with all solar states, no special-callsign or moving-station exclusions, and 0 dB Reference SNR correction. Time intervals in this guide include their start and exclude their end.

For ΔSNR, a qualifying station has retained paired observations over the full filtered period. This retains sparsely observed paths; it does not make a single observation representative of the whole month.

Two statistical terms help explain the map settings. The arithmetic mean is the sum of the paired SNR differences divided by the number of pairs. A 95% confidence interval (CI) describes uncertainty in that estimated mean under the calculation’s assumptions—not the range of individual receptions. The ± value gives the CI half-width: how far the interval extends on either side of the mean.

The ΔSNR map colors a segment only with at least five qualifying stations and a calculable 95% mean CI with a positive half-width no greater than 0.5 dB. These display requirements do not remove eligible pairs from Inspector statistics.

ΔDecode has separate support rules: at least one observed station and 1% of the full run’s observed events, or 1 256 events here. Each event is one transmitter identity in one WSPR cycle reported by at least one receiver. This requirement limits coloring based on very small amounts of data; it is not a confidence statement.

ΔSNR (Signal Quality): where is the SNR advantage?

Geographic scope Pooled mean ΔSNR ± 95% CI Qualifying stations Spot pairs
E, 0–2 500 km +6.82 ± 0.09 dB 172 19 205
WNW, 5 000–10 000 km +0.08 ± 0.16 dB 113 2 911

SNR describes how strongly a signal stands out from noise. Here, ΔSNR means G3ZIL SNR − G4HZX SNR: −22 minus −29 dB gives +7 dB, favoring G3ZIL. Negative differences favor G4HZX.

The map is centered on G3ZIL. Each sector represents a compass direction, and each ring a distance range. The colors show the average SNR difference between G3ZIL and G4HZX for transmissions from the same transmitter decoded by both stations in the same WSPR cycle (paired). Every paired reception counts equally, so frequently heard transmitters have more influence on the average.

Compare the nearby eastern paths with the more distant WNW paths.

The first visible finding is that G3ZIL’s advantage is not geographically uniform. Many nearer European sectors favor G3ZIL clearly, while the distant west-northwest sector containing many North American stations is much closer to equality.

In that distant WNW sector, the average difference is only +0.08 dB. Its 95% confidence interval extends from about −0.08 to +0.24 dB: from a small average advantage for G4HZX to a small average advantage for G3ZIL. The strong advantage seen on nearer eastern paths is therefore not apparent in this distant sector. This does not prove equal performance, and the interval describes the average—not the differences on individual transmissions.

Markers answer a different question from segment colors. Paired (qualifying) marks a station supplying enough same-cycle pairs. Both observed, no pairs means both receivers reported that transmitter during April, but never in the same cycle; it does not imply a timing fault. Only G3ZIL and Only G4HZX mark stations reported exclusively by that receiver. A paired station can also supply one-sided observations in other cycles.

Hatching means insufficient display support, not no reception. Select a hatched segment and check its qualifying-station count and the Spots CI in Benchmark Statistics. Missing or zero-width intervals also prevent coloring. Both examples in the table meet the display requirements. The legend names gray diagonal hatching Not qualifying. Unfilled segments (No observations) have no retained observations within the selected scope and filters; this does not establish physical absence of reception. Qualifying 0 dB values remain colored. The Map shading: footer states the applicable requirements. Neutral colors cover a range around parity, not exact equality; read the numerical legend. Restore Full Range | All Directions before continuing.

What is missing from ΔSNR? ΔSNR requires both receivers to decode the same signal in the same WSPR cycle. When only one receiver reports a decode, no paired SNR difference can be calculated—even though that reception may represent a meaningful advantage.

One-sided decodes can occur near the WSPR detection limit, but they can also occur at stronger reported SNRs. Differences in antennas, propagation paths, local noise, interference, decoding or reporting may explain why a signal appears in one receiver’s reports but not the other’s.

Read left to right as the SNR cutoff—the minimum reported SNR included in the count—falls in 1 dB steps. Progressively weaker successful reports enter the curves. Spots are above, distinct station identities below. Each receiver’s curve uses its raw reported SNR, without power normalization or Reference correction; duplicate reports for one receiver, transmitter and cycle use the strongest raw SNR.

The Paired spots curves end at the same count because they describe the same transmissions. One receiver’s report can enter at a higher SNR cutoff than the other’s. Their separation before the shared endpoint therefore reflects different SNRs, not additional decodes.

The middle panels distinguish two kinds of extra reception. The One-sided spots panel counts all reports without a same-cycle counterpart. The Exclusive stations panel counts transmitters reported by only one receiver during the whole selected period. A one-sided spot is not necessarily a new station: the same transmitter can supply pairs in some cycles and one-sided reports in others.

Use the Spots and Stations reconciliation panels to distinguish receiver totals from Union (Either), the combined set in which each shared event or identity counts once.

The 35 shared but unpaired identities are already included in both receiver totals; their spots are one-sided. They are annotated separately in Exclusive stations, not added to its two exclusive-station curves. Intermediate station-curve counts need not add: Stations with paired spots uses each receiver’s strongest paired report, while All observed stations uses its strongest report of any outcome.

Most additional reception came from already-observed paths: 54 030 of G3ZIL’s 56 260 one-sided events (96%) came from identities that also supplied pairs. The practical benefit was therefore mainly more reception on shared paths, not simply a larger list of transmitters. Yet both receivers added exclusive paths, including 49 identities heard only by G4HZX.

One-sided reports were typically weaker. Their median—the middle SNR after ordering the reports—was −19 dB, versus −12 dB for G3ZIL’s paired reports; at G4HZX the corresponding values were −23 versus −19 dB. This is consistent with extra signal margin helping reception, but the broad SNR range does not define a decoding threshold. The absent receiver’s SNR and reason for the missing report remain unknown.

We now know that the totals differ. The next map asks where those extra decodes occurred, and whether their pattern follows the paired-SNR map.

05

ΔDecode (Decoding Advantage): where are additional signals decoded?

The decoding advantage varies geographically too—and it does not always follow the paired-SNR advantage. Compare this map with the ΔSNR map, using the same directions and distances.

ΔDecode compares observed decode totals, including paired and one-sided reports. Here it is displayed as a factor: 2× means twice as many observed decodes for the indicated receiver. The map pools all decodes in a segment; it does not average station ratios. Warm colors favor G3ZIL, blue favors G4HZX. The central band extends to 1.25× in either direction, so its color does not imply equality.

E and SSW occupy the same 1.5×–1.75× band but add very different decode volumes. A similar relative advantage can mean many more receptions on a busy path than on a less frequently observed one. Distant WNW is close to parity, although similar totals do not mean the receivers reported exactly the same transmissions.

NE is the useful counterexample: G3ZIL has better SNR on the transmissions both receivers reported, while G4HZX has more total decodes. There is no contradiction: paired ΔSNR excludes one-sided reception, whereas ΔDecode includes it. Use both maps rather than treating the SNR map as a prediction of decoding success.

Eleven segments meet the separate station and 1 256-event requirements. That threshold remains tied to the full run when you change Inspector scope. Hatching retains inspectable evidence below the display minimum. Interpret count ratios under comparable operation and reporting; they are not absolute decoding probabilities.

Read Benchmark Statistics: how large is the difference?

Restore Segment Inspector → Full Range | All Directions. Compare the upper-left spot-pair distribution with the upper-right station-means distribution and their matching Spots and Stations cards.

G3ZIL’s average advantage remains about 6–7 dB when each transmitting-station identity is given equal influence. This is a useful check because some transmitters supplied far more pairs than others.

The pooled mean gives every spot pair equal weight. The mean of station means first calculates the average difference for each station, then averages those station results equally. A frequently heard station therefore has more influence on the first summary, but no more influence than another station on the second.

Both weightings support the same broad advantage. The histograms also show substantial variation, including observations favoring G4HZX: the monthly mean is not what every path experienced. The paired median +7.0 dB is a complementary summary. The lower-right median of station medians, +6.5 dB, takes the middle value of the individual stations’ medians; neither median is a mean.

We use Student-t confidence intervals as a practical summary of the mean’s estimated precision: the calculation accounts for both the number of observations and their variability. The ± value describes uncertainty in the mean, not the spread of individual reports. For example, +6.55 ± 0.05 dB corresponds to an interval of about +6.50 to +6.60 dB; it does not say that individual paired differences lie in that narrow range. Here, both weighting approaches support the same clear advantage. Likewise, the earlier regional intervals describe each region separately; they are not a statistical test of the difference between regions.

The lower-left ΔDecode - Spot Decodes histogram answers a different question. Its bars show the share of combined receiver decodes contributed by stations with each decode ratio, not the percentage of stations. Its 183 285 decodes count each pair twice, once per receiver; the observed-event union counts it once. The line marks the pooled G3ZIL 1.65× result. Exclusive endpoints contain exclusively heard stations; large finite ratios stay inside, and shared stations can still supply one-sided events.

Substantial volume favors G3ZIL on shared paths, matching the Decoding Reach finding. About 70% of station identities supplied pairs, but only 46% of observed events were paired: reading both metrics keeps the remaining reception in view.

Open Station Insights: do individual paths agree?

Station Insights browses the full analysis independently of the Inspector. Leave Filter by selected segment and distance off; enable Include one-sided evidence to also list identities without pairs. The segment filter narrows the station list, not the selected station’s evidence: that still covers its full April record, even if the station falls outside the list filter.

Select DJ0ABR (JN68nt), prepared in the demo. This transmitter is useful because one path shows both extra reception with an uncertain paired-SNR advantage and a clear SNR advantage with no extra decodes. It makes the difference between signal margin and additional reception tangible.

Here, better SNR means more signal margin, not more observed decodes.
