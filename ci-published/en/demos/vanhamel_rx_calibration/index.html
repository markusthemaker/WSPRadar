One Antenna, Two Receivers — Why Calibration Matters

Calibrate your receivers before comparing antennas

Two receivers connected to one antenna should tell a similar story. Use WSPRadar to find the difference introduced by the receiving equipment—before mistaking it for an antenna advantage.

What you will take away: a receiving-chain offset of +1.22 ± 0.03 dB (mean and 95% confidence interval), supported by 1 450 spot pairs from 50 station entries, and how to apply the rounded +1.2 dB Reference correction in the next antenna benchmark. This value belongs to the receiver pair in this demo; your own pair needs its own calibration.

01

Setup: how were the receiving chains compared?

Before comparing two antennas, Jurgen Vanhamel, Walter Machiels and Hervé Lamy checked the receiving equipment itself. Their paper describes two receiving chains connected to the same long-wire antenna through a splitter, using matching processing settings. This RX Benchmark compares the database reports from Target ON4AWM0, the chain being evaluated, with Reference ON4AWM1, the chain used for comparison.

Each receiving chain includes more than the receiver: cables, filters and signal-processing settings also belong to the setup. With both chains fed by the same antenna, a persistent SNR difference provides a baseline for the equipment rather than a comparison between antennas.

Our question is: how large is that difference, and is there enough evidence to use it as a calibration baseline?

SNR is the signal-to-noise ratio: how strongly a signal stands out relative to noise. Higher SNR is better. WSPRadar finds spot pairs: occasions when both receivers report the same transmitter with the same reported locator in the same two-minute WSPR cycle. This lets us compare their SNR for the same transmission. Signals reported by only one receiver cannot provide this paired difference.

A station identity is a transmitter’s callsign together with its full reported locator. A spot pair consists of one retained Target observation and one retained Reference observation matched on band, WSPR cycle and station identity. Each pair supplies one SNR difference.

When several reports exist for one setup, remote station, and cycle, WSPRadar consolidates them using its existing SNR-selection rules before forming the comparison.

ΔSNR is the Target SNR minus the Reference SNR. For this demo, no correction is applied:

ΔSNR = ON4AWM0 SNR − ON4AWM1 SNR

Positive values mean ON4AWM0 reported the higher SNR; negative values mean ON4AWM1 did. Zero means equal reported SNR.

Find the receiver offset, check its support, and prepare for an antenna comparison.

03

Read the result and check its support

What is the typical difference?

Start with the upper-left ΔSNR ± 95% CI - Qualifying spot pairs panel.

The primary calibration estimate is +1.22 ± 0.03 dB at 95% confidence: ON4AWM0 reports the higher average SNR. The pooled mean is the arithmetic average across all 1 450 retained spot pairs. Every pair contributes equally, so frequently heard transmitters have more influence. This is the offset estimate we will use for the correction.

Next, check the upper-right ΔSNR ± 95% CI - Qualifying station means panel. Its +1.24 ± 0.13 dB result gives each of the 50 station identities equal influence. The mean of station means first averages the paired differences for each transmitter, then averages those station results equally. The offset barely changes when frequently heard stations no longer have extra weight. This is a useful check using the same observations, not a new calibration run. Its CI describes the station-weighted mean, not an adjusted version of the pooled CI.

Finally, use the lower-right ΔSNR - Qualifying station medians panel as a brief cross-check. A median is the middle value of an ordered set of observations. The pooled median and the median of the 50 station medians are both +1.00 dB. Most paired differences lie at +1 or +2 dB, so the mean is slightly higher. Both summaries indicate a positive offset, but the mean remains the calibration estimate.

Is there enough evidence behind it?

50 station identities contribute 1 450 retained pairs, with 1–344 pairs per identity. The result is therefore repeated across different transmitters, rather than resting on a single signal or a handful of reports.

The paper selected stations received more than 50 times during seven days. This demo includes every station identity with at least one retained spot pair and assesses the mean’s estimated precision using a 95% confidence interval (CI). A fixed observation-count cutoff alone cannot specify that precision: the variability of the paired differences matters too.1

We use Student-t confidence intervals as a practical summary of the mean’s estimated precision: the calculation accounts for both the number of observations and their variability. The ± value describes uncertainty in the mean, not the spread of individual reports. Here, both weighting approaches support a positive receiving-chain offset of about +1.2 dB.

Calculated before rounding, the pooled 95% CI runs from approximately +1.19 to +1.25 dB. Its ±0.03 dB half-width is tight relative to the offset. The +1.22 dB estimate rounds to +1.2 dB, matching the paper’s reported average at its displayed precision. The interval stays positive, supporting the rounded +1.2 dB Reference correction for this calibration. This is the estimated precision of the mean in the selected data, not a claim of absolute equipment accuracy.

The demo’s map precision limit is ±0.1 dB at 95% confidence. It controls whether a map segment is colored; segments with a wider pooled-pair CI remain hatched. This limit does not filter the observations used in Benchmark Statistics or the temporal plot, and it does not change the measured ±0.03 dB.

The lower-left ΔDecode - Spot Decodes panel answers a separate question: which chain recorded more decodes? It includes all 59 observed station entries, including stations without a retained spot pair. Its 3 231 decodes are the two receivers' counts added together; a shared transmission contributes one decode to each receiver. This broader decoding population should not be confused with the 50 stations used to estimate the SNR offset.

The statistics give us a consistent offset estimate across the two weightings. Before using one correction for the whole experiment, check whether the positive offset persists through the week. We will then return to the decoding result to see what an SNR correction cannot fix.

Does the offset persist through the week?

Open Segment Inspector → Temporal Evidence → ΔSNR over Time and keep Full Range | All Directions, Time basis: UTC and ΔSNR statistic: Mean. Select 12 h chronological bins to match the supplied figure; the prepared configuration otherwise leaves this bin selection on automatic.

The offset remains positive throughout the sampled week, although its size and precision vary. All fourteen twelve-hour means and their individual 95% confidence intervals are above zero. The means range from +1.04 to +1.58 dB: the receiving-chain difference persists, but is not exactly constant.

The left panel follows successive half-days; the right combines observations from the same one-hour UTC interval across all dates. Points show each bin's mean, and the dashed line marks the whole-run pooled mean of +1.22 dB. The shaded band is each bin's own 95% CI, not the spread of individual reports or a simultaneous confidence band for the whole curve. The whole-run ±0.03 dB does not apply to every time bin. Background colors show relative spot-pair density; read the labeled dB values on the nonlinear vertical scale.

In the app’s temporal ΔSNR figures, both panels share a range derived from the contributing observations and density-bin edges, with display padding; wide CIs do not enlarge it. A hollow △ at the upper plot edge or ▽ at the lower plot edge marks a bin whose CI bound continues beyond that edge. Both markers appear if both bounds extend beyond the range. The figure footer shows △ ▽ CI extends beyond displayed range only if at least one CI extends beyond the range. The numerical CI bounds remain unchanged.

For your own calibration, investigate a sustained shift before adopting one correction for the whole run. Here, the positive baseline persists in the available paired evidence. The remaining question is whether both chains also report the same signals—something paired SNR alone cannot answer.

Does correcting SNR also equalize decoding?

An SNR correction aligns reported levels, but it does not make the two chains decode equally. Here, ON4AWM0 recorded more signals, with most of its additional reports near the weak end of the scale. One-sided decodes are reports recorded by only one chain; they add reception evidence that cannot supply a paired ΔSNR. Correcting the reported SNR offset cannot recover a missed decode.

For a final check, open Global View → Decodes & Stations by SNR Cutoff. Follow the curves from stronger signals on the left toward weaker signals on the right. They progressively include lower-SNR successful reports, showing how the totals of decoded spots and distinct transmitting stations build up for each receiver. These are cumulative counts: a steep rise means many decodes fall within a narrow reported-SNR range.

The top-middle panel shows 316 ON4AWM0-only decodes and 15 ON4AWM1-only decodes, concentrated near the weak end of the scale, around −30 dB. The top-left Paired spots panel represents 1 450 spot pairs, with 1 450 retained observations from each receiver, the same paired population used for the calibration SNR summaries.

The concentration is striking: 293 of the 316 Target-only decodes—about 93%—fall between −28 and −32 dB raw reported SNR. Near the decoding limit, a small real SNR improvement can determine whether a WSPR message is decoded at all. When many signals sit in this region, the number of successful decodes can change substantially. WSPR’s decoding threshold represents a transition in decoding probability, rather than a hard boundary applying to every reception. WSJT-X User Guide This pattern is consistent with marginal-signal decoding; the measured +1.2 dB reporting offset alone does not prove the cause.

This is why ΔDecode complements ΔSNR. ΔSNR compares only transmissions decoded by both receivers. ΔDecode compares their total observed decodes—Paired plus each receiver’s own one-sided decodes—and therefore captures reception missing from the paired comparison. Here, 1 766 versus 1 465 decodes gives 1.21× as many observed decodes, with a net advantage of 316 − 15 = 301.

The bottom row shows 59 station entries at ON4AWM0 versus 50 at ON4AWM1. The advantage includes nine transmitting-station entries absent from the Reference’s log, as well as additional transmissions from stations both receivers heard.

An SNR correction adjusts the reported values used in the comparison. It cannot recover a missed decode or establish equal decoding performance. These curves use each receiver’s raw reported SNR, so the Reference correction does not move them. Interpret one-sided evidence under known common operation and reporting: outages or missing uploads can also produce a difference. The counts do not measure an absolute decoding probability or identify the physical cause of the imbalance.

Keep this decoding result with your calibration record. The next antenna benchmark can correct the +1.2 dB SNR offset but may still include differences in decoding performance between the receiving chains. The 1.21× factor describes this run; it is not a correction to apply to later decode counts.

04

Use the result in the next antenna benchmark

Calibration result: In the selected week of database records, ON4AWM0 reported a mean SNR difference of +1.22 ± 0.03 dB at 95% confidence relative to ON4AWM1. The result is supported by 1 450 same-cycle comparisons from 50 transmitting-station entries. With both receiving chains connected to the same antenna, such an offset is a receiver-chain baseline, not an antenna advantage; the database alone does not verify that connection for the entire week.

To account for this offset, a +1.2 dB Reference SNR correction raises ON4AWM1's SNR before subtraction, bringing the average difference close to zero. The prepared calibration demo stays at 0.0 dB, so the original offset remains visible.

Where to enter the correction

Save the calibration results. Connect the test antenna to Target ON4AWM0 and the comparison antenna to Reference ON4AWM1. Retain the calibrated band and receiver settings, with both receivers reporting throughout.

In RX Benchmark → Reference Setup/Station, select the experiment dates, choose Use a documented correction, enter +1.2 in Reference-side SNR correction (dB), and run.

The positive correction is added to Reference:

Corrected ΔSNR = Target SNR − (Reference SNR + 1.2 dB)

For other receiving chains, use your own measured offset. Recheck after equipment, band or settings changes. Reversing Target and Reference reverses the correction sign.
