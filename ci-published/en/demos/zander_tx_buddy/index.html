Short Portable Vertical vs Larger Vertical

How much performance do you trade for portability?

Two transmitting antennas, the same remote receivers: use WSPRadar to compare their signals, see where the difference changes, and check the observations behind the result. Follow the signal from Europe to North America, then use the time charts and one receiver to understand what the numbers mean.

Throughout this guided demo, ΔSNR compares signal quality when the same receiver decoded both transmissions in the same cycle, while ΔDecode compares observed report counts, including reception of only one setup. Together, they help distinguish signal margin, additional reports and the number of receivers reached.

01

What were the operators testing?

Can a short portable vertical compete with a larger antenna on the same radio paths?

Prof. Jens Zander’s experiment compared two transmitting antennas on Gotland, Sweden. Each antenna was connected to a nominal 200 mW WSPR transmitter from ZachTek, with both beacons configured to transmit during the same two-minute cycles on 20 meters.

  • SK0WE/P — Target: a Difona HF-P1, a 2.5 m center-loaded portable vertical with four radials.
  • SK0WE/1 — Reference: a Hy-Gain AV620, the larger vertical used for comparison.

In this TX Benchmark, Target and Reference are the two transmitting setups: the installation being evaluated and the installation used for comparison. The stations reporting their signals are the global remote receivers. A station identity identifies a receiver by its callsign and full reported locator. A spot is an uploaded reception report following a successful decode; repeated spots add observations without necessarily adding another receiver identity.

WSPRadar forms a spot pair when the same receiving-station identity reports both transmitters on the same band in the same two-minute WSPR cycle. It retains one report per setup after consolidating duplicates using its SNR-selection rules. Comparing at the same receiver and time avoids mixing reports from different receiving setups or different parts of the day. Each pair supplies one SNR difference.

A Target-only observation means a receiver reported SK0WE/P but not SK0WE/1 in that cycle; Reference-only means the reverse. These one-sided observations still show reception, but there is no measured SNR for the missing report to subtract.

The quantity we compare is signal-to-noise ratio, or SNR: how strongly a signal stands out relative to noise. Higher SNR is better. The difference, ΔSNR, is:

ΔSNR = SK0WE/P SNR − SK0WE/1 SNR

Positive values favor the short Difona vertical; negative values favor the larger Hy-Gain vertical. Zero means equal SNR in that comparison.

Our question is: how much difference did the receivers observe, and was that difference the same on every path? The decoding views add a second question: which transmissions were reported from just one setup?

The analysis covers 20 m on 21 May 2022, 09:52–10:20 UTC, with the end excluded: 28 minutes. It includes all directions within 10 000 km, all solar states, no special-callsign or moving-station exclusions, and 0 dB Reference SNR correction. Time intervals throughout this guide include their start and exclude their end.

A qualifying receiver has retained paired observations across the comparison period. This retains sparsely sampled paths; it does not make one observation a stable estimate. A ΔSNR map segment needs at least one qualifying receiver and an available 95% mean confidence interval with a positive half-width no greater than 1 dB before it is colored.

A confidence interval (CI) describes uncertainty in an estimated mean under the calculation's statistical assumptions, not the spread of individual observations. Its half-width is the amount after ±. One pair alone cannot provide this mean CI. The ±1 dB limit controls map coloring only; wider intervals and the underlying pairs remain available in other views.

The ΔDecode map uses separate requirements: one observed receiver and 1% of the run's observed receiver-cycle events, rounded up to five events here. An observed event is one receiver identity and WSPR cycle with a report of at least one transmitter; a pair counts once for this requirement. These are count requirements, not confidence statements.

Read the ΔSNR Map: is the difference the same everywhere?

Open Global View → ΔSNR Map.

The map is centered on Target SK0WE/P on Gotland. Sectors represent compass directions, rings represent distance ranges, and their intersections form map segments. The markers show the remote receivers that reported the transmissions.

Each colored segment shows the average SNR difference for cycles in which the same receiver reported both transmitters. Every pair counts equally, so frequently reporting receivers have more influence on the average. Negative values and blue shading favor the larger vertical, Reference SK0WE/1.

Start with the European receivers within 2 500 km of the center. The larger vertical has the higher average paired SNR in all three colored segments, but its advantage varies with direction. These segments—SSW, SW and WSW—are the only three of the ten observed segments that meet the map's coloring requirements.

Use Segment Inspector to compare two of them. Its Spots mean and CI use the same pairs and weighting as the selected map segment:

The larger vertical's average advantage is nearly 10 dB toward SSW Europe, compared with about 5 dB toward WSW Europe. The short vertical's signal-quality disadvantage is therefore not the same on every sampled European path.

Now extend the comparison across the Atlantic. In Segment Inspector, select 5 000–10 000 km | WNW to examine the paths to receivers in the USA. This segment is hatched, so its SNR result must be read in the Inspector rather than inferred from a segment color.

The Inspector retains 10 spot pairs from three qualifying receivers, with a mean of −5.30 ± 2.39 dB. The short vertical reached these US receivers, but the larger vertical delivered the higher average paired SNR. The interval corresponds to an average Reference advantage of about 2.9–7.7 dB. Its ±2.39 dB half-width exceeds the map's ±1 dB limit, explaining why the segment is not colored even though the interval favors Reference throughout.

Hatching means the coloring requirements are not met—not that reception was absent. For other hatched segments, check the qualifying-receiver count and Spots interval: there may be a wide interval or no pairs from which to calculate one. Blank segments have no observed receiver identities. Neither appearance establishes that an antenna radiates nothing in that direction. Eligible observations remain in Inspector statistics; the export retains the exact qualification reasons and unrounded intervals.

These regional results describe the sampled paths, not a fixed antenna radiation pattern. Their confidence intervals describe each region separately; they are not a statistical test of the difference between regions.

Marker colors describe reception categories; segment colors describe SNR differences. Green markers identify receivers with qualifying pairs; Only SK0WE/P and Only SK0WE/1 identify receivers that reported only one setup during this run. A receiver with pairs can also contribute one-sided reports in other cycles.

What is missing from ΔSNR? When a receiver reports only one transmitter in a cycle, no paired difference can be calculated—even though that reception may matter. The next view includes these observations and shows whether the signal-quality advantage accompanies more decodes and wider receiver reach. Restore Full Range | All Directions before continuing.

04

Open Decodes by SNR: what else was heard?

The larger vertical was reported more often and by more receivers—not just with better SNR on shared observations. One-sided reports supply the reception evidence missing from the paired comparison. Their counts also include the differing reporting spans, which we will examine in the time charts.

In Segment Inspector, open Decodes by SNR, retaining Full Range | All Directions. Follow the curves from stronger signals on the left toward weaker signals on the right. These are cumulative curves: each point includes reports at the displayed SNR cutoff or stronger. Moving right lowers that cutoff in 1 dB steps and adds weaker reports. The upper row counts spots; the lower row counts distinct receiver identities. Use the Target · SK0WE/P and Reference · SK0WE/1 labels to follow each setup.

The curves use raw reported SNR, without transmit-power normalization or Reference correction. The upper-left Paired spots curves finish at the same count because each pair contains one report of each transmitter. Before the endpoint, Reference rises earlier: its paired reports tend to have higher SNR, not additional decodes within that panel.

The larger vertical reached 49 more receiver identities overall. Its 50 exclusive receivers represent additional paths, not merely repeated reports from the shared receivers. Conversely, one receiver reported only the short vertical. A one-sided spot is not necessarily an exclusive receiver: the same receiver may report both transmitters in other cycles.

Union, labeled Either, counts each observed receiver-cycle or identity once, even when both transmitters were reported. Both observed, no pairs: +0 stations means there are no additional receivers that reported both setups only in different cycles. Each station curve uses the strongest report relevant to that panel, so the lower panels need not add at every intermediate SNR cutoff, even though their final populations reconcile.

Read the endpoints together with the Spots and Stations reconciliation panels:

Extra signal margin can help a weak path produce a decode rather than no report. Reference's one-sided reports tend to be weaker than its paired reports, consistent with that benefit. However, one-sided reports are not confined to a single detection threshold. Propagation, interference, transmission schedules, reception and reporting gaps can also affect which reports appear; the missing side's SNR is unknown.

The full-window ratio—about 2.3× as many decodes for Reference—is a comparison of observed counts, not an absolute transmission-success probability. The next map asks where those additional reports came from, rather than assuming that every direction benefits equally.

05

Read the ΔDecode Map: where were more reports recorded?

Open Global View → ΔDecode Map. Here, ΔDecode compares each setup's total observed decodes, including pairs and one-sided reports. It pools those counts within each segment rather than averaging receiver ratios. 2× means twice as many observed decodes, not twice the signal strength or twice as many receivers.

The larger vertical's count advantage also varies geographically, but it does not follow a fixed conversion from dB to extra decodes.

Warm colors favor Target and blue colors favor Reference. The central band extends to 1.25× in either direction, so it does not imply exact equality. Exclusive means that only one setup has reports in that segment; it is an observed result, not proof that the other setup could never be received there.

Eight of ten observed segments meet this map's one-receiver and five-event minimums. Nearby NW and S each have four events and remain hatched. Unlike ΔSNR, this map can color segments with no pairs. Its requirements do not use the ±1 dB SNR limit.

Compare these segments:

SSW shows a larger paired-SNR advantage than SW, yet SW has the larger decode ratio. WSW and distant WNW have similar mean SNR differences but different count ratios. Signal quality and observed decoding therefore describe related, not interchangeable, aspects of reception. These counts retain the full-window reporting-span limitation.

We have now seen where signal quality and decoding differ. Benchmark Statistics brings the paired results together and asks whether the overall conclusion depends on how receivers are weighted.

06

Read Benchmark Statistics: how large is the difference?

Open Segment Inspector → Benchmark Statistics, with Full Range | All Directions selected.

The larger vertical's paired-SNR advantage remains when each receiver is given equal weight. All 37 qualifying receiver means are negative. The result is therefore not just a consequence of a few frequently reporting receivers dominating the pooled average.

The pooled mean averages all paired differences together. The mean of receiver means first averages each receiver's paired differences, then gives those receiver averages equal weight. For example, ON5KQ contributes 11 pairs with a mean of −8.18 dB, while N2HQI (FN13sa) contributes three with a mean of −3.33 dB. Their contributions are eleven versus three observations in the first summary, but one receiver mean each in the second.

The histograms group similar values into bars; bar height shows the share of observations or receivers in each range. Compare the upper-left spot-pair panel with the upper-right ΔSNR ± 95% CI - Qualifying station means panel and their matching Spots and Stations cards:

Summary Mean ΔSNR ± 95% CI What receives equal weight?
Pooled spot-pair mean −6.78 ± 0.54 dB Each of 166 spot pairs
Mean of receiver means −6.26 ± 0.93 dB Each of 37 receiver means

Both summaries indicate an average advantage of roughly 6–7 dB for Reference. They differ by only about half a decibel. Their agreement shows that changing the weighting leaves the broad conclusion intact; they are two summaries of the same evidence, not independent experiments. Neither weighting is automatically more accurate: one summarizes the sampled comparisons, the other the sampled receivers.

Read the spread as well as the mean. Individual pairs vary considerably; a few tie or favor Target even though every receiver's mean favors Reference. The median is the middle value after sorting. The spot-pair median −7 dB and lower-right median of receiver medians −5 dB remain useful secondary summaries. All 37 receiver medians are negative too; the median panel does not display a median CI.

How precise is the average?

We use Student-t confidence intervals as a practical summary of the mean’s estimated precision: the calculation accounts for both the number of observations and their variability. The ± value describes uncertainty in the mean, not the spread of individual reports. Here, both weighting approaches support the same clear advantage.

All 166 retained pairs contribute, including pairs in hatched map segments. Thirteen receivers supply only one or two pairs each. Giving them equal weight does not make their estimates robust. For your own experiment, sample the same paths repeatedly over longer common operating periods, keeping installations and power unchanged. More data can clarify consistency; it does not guarantee that differently weighted summaries converge. Changing map-coloring requirements alone does not remove retained paired evidence from Inspector statistics. The shared confidence level affects mean CIs across Benchmark views.

For your own comparisons, use this reading guide:

If the summaries… How to interpret them
Are close and favor the same setup The observation- and receiver-level results tell a similar story; still check coverage and variation.
Differ but favor the same setup The estimated advantage depends on weighting or summary choice; inspect pair counts, directions and periods.
Favor different setups Investigate which paths and periods favor each setup before declaring an overall winner.

The lower-left ΔDecode - Spot Decodes histogram answers a different question: where does the decode volume sit? It weights receivers by their contributed decodes, not equally. Its 659 decodes count a paired event twice, once per transmitter; the 493-event union counts it once. Most volume lies on the Reference side. Reference Exclusive contains receivers that never reported Target, not every Reference-only report. The dashed line marks the pooled Reference advantage, shown as 2.29× in the figure—about 2.3×.

The overall SNR conclusion is consistent across these summaries. The optional publication comparison checks that broader finding; the time views then ask whether it persisted through the test and whether decode counts cover comparable reporting periods.

Open Station Insights: what did one receiver observe?

The Station Insights table moves from the overall comparison to a particular receiver. Its list covers the full analysis by default. Filter by selected segment and distance narrows the list; selected-station evidence still covers that receiver's full run. Include one-sided evidence also lists receivers without pairs.

Select ON5KQ at JO10os, about 1 267 km from Target at an azimuth of 241°. It is not preselected by the preset. Open its ΔSNR over Time and check its independent settings: Time aggregation: 2m, Time basis: UTC, ΔSNR statistic: Mean.

Every paired observation at ON5KQ favors the larger vertical, but the signal difference changes between cycles. Its eleven pairs range from −12 to −6 dB; the dashed full-window mean is −8.18 dB. This receiver is useful because it supplies both repeated pairs and one-sided reports, letting us compare signal margin and decoding on the same path.

Why is there no confidence band around these markers?

Each of the eleven populated two-minute bins contains exactly one pair. Its mean is simply that pair's difference. With no within-bin spread to estimate, a mean CI cannot be calculated; there is therefore no CI band or CI legend. This does not mean zero uncertainty.

The separate full-window calculation across all eleven pairs gives −8.18 ± 1.47 dB. That aggregate CI is not a per-bin band in the figure. The number of markers follows the time grouping; the number of pairs behind each marker tells you how much information supports it.

The hourly panel remains unavailable for the same one-date reason as before. Now compare the eleven negative SNR differences with what ON5KQ actually decoded.

What does its ΔDecode view add?

Open the selected station’s ΔDecode over Time, retaining 2m and UTC.

Despite lower Target SNR in every paired observation, ON5KQ reported the short vertical slightly more often: 13 decodes versus 12. The eleven pairs contribute equally to both totals; the difference comes from two Target-only cycles and one Reference-only cycle.

The Target-only cycles are 09:52 and 10:04 UTC; Reference-only is 10:18. The green paired cycles show 1× because both transmissions were decoded, regardless of their SNR difference. The one-sided cycles appear at the respective Exclusive endpoint, and cannot supply a paired SNR difference.

A weaker signal can still be decoded successfully. Here, greater Reference signal margin did not produce more observed decodes at this receiver. Conversely, the one-decode Target lead does not establish better reliability: reporting opportunity and the cause of absent reports remain unknown. The one-sided boundaries also show why the chosen time window matters.

The two views answer different questions rather than contradicting each other. Drill-Down lets you check the actual SNR subtraction in cycles where both signals were decoded.

Use Drill-Down: check an individual comparison

With ON5KQ selected, open Drill-Down and inspect the time and the two SNR columns. Even a 12 dB relative disadvantage can coexist with successful decoding of both transmissions, as the first example shows.

These paired examples come from the retained selected-station observations. The selected-station export referenced for this demo is benchmark/table_drilldown_selected_stations.csv:

09:54 −13 dB −1 dB −12 dB
10:02 −11 dB −5 dB −6 dB
10:12 −5 dB +1 dB −6 dB

At 09:54, −13 − (−1) = −12 dB. Reference had the higher SNR, but both signals were decoded. At 10:02 and 10:12 the difference is the same despite different SNR levels on both sides. A relative difference alone therefore does not tell you how far either signal stands above noise.

These SNR values are power-normalized to 30 dBm (1 W): adjusted from the reported transmit power to a common comparison basis. The earlier cumulative curves use raw reports instead. In these rows both sides receive the same +7 dB adjustment, leaving ΔSNR unchanged. No additional Reference correction is applied. An absent report has no measured SNR; it must not be treated as a zero-dB signal.

You do not need to inspect every row. Use Drill-Down to verify the arithmetic, understand an unusual observation or check what contributed to a summary. These examples complete the chain from the geographic pattern to individual reports; finish by stating the result and preserving the evidence.

11

State the result—and preserve it

In this 20 m test, the larger Hy-Gain AV620 provided higher paired SNR across the sampled receiving paths. The 166 spot pairs give a mean ΔSNR of −6.78 ± 0.54 dB; giving each of the 37 receiver means equal weight gives −6.26 ± 0.93 dB. All receiver means favor the larger vertical, while the size of the difference varies across paths and cycles.

The short vertical still reached North America. Being heard, matching a larger antenna’s SNR and accumulating more decodes are different questions. Reference led in full-window decode totals and receiver reach, but the reporting spans were not identical. At ON5KQ, its stronger paired signals even coexisted with one fewer observed decode.

For a portable operator, this is the useful trade-off: the compact antenna can still reach distant stations, while the larger installation provides more signal margin on the sampled paths. Use the maps to examine the directions that matter to your operating, then check whether the margin is accompanied by more reports under comparable operation.

This result describes these installed transmitting setups during the selected 28-minute period. It is not a fixed penalty for every portable vertical or a measurement of antenna gain in every direction.
