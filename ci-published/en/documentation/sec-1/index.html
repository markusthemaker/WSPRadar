Part 0: Why WSPRadar

You change an antenna, feedline, receiver or station setting. Did it help — and where, when and by how much? A stronger report or extra WSPR spot is a useful observation, but propagation, remote-station activity and local noise may have changed too.

WSPRadar helps investigate that question across many radio paths. It analyzes WSPR reception reports to show geographic reach, signal differences and decoding consistency over time. You can follow a map pattern down to the stations and individual reports behind it.

Repeated or simultaneous comparisons provide a stronger basis than unrelated reports collected hours apart. Published WSPR experiments demonstrate the value of comparable operating conditions [Ref-1] [Ref-2] [Ref-3] [Ref-4] [Ref-5].

WSPRadar describes complete stations and documented signal paths under real operating conditions. It does not directly measure antenna gain, efficiency or receiver sensitivity. To attribute a difference to one component, control the other differences and repeat the comparison.

0.1 WSPR in 2 Minutes

WSPR means Weak Signal Propagation Reporter. Low-power stations transmit timed digital signals that other stations decode and report. A WSPR cycle is a two-minute interval beginning at an even UTC minute [Ref-6] [Ref-8].

A spot is a report of a successful decode. It records the transmitter and receiver identities and locations, time, band, reported transmit power and SNR — signal-to-noise ratio in dB. Higher SNR means a stronger signal relative to noise; a negative value does not mean decoding failed.

WSPRadar analyzes uploaded reports; it does not receive or decode radio signals. WSPRnet displays reception reports. WSPRadar normally reads wspr.live, with WSPRDaemon WD2 and WD1 as alternatives; each run uses one database without combining sources. See data availability and source selection [Ref-10] [Ref-11].

Reports show successful decodes, not every attempted transmission or listening receiver. A missing spot alone does not prove a failed reception. How WSPRadar handles that uncertainty depends on the analysis you choose.

0.2 What WSPRadar can show

The Target is the station or signal path you want to investigate. A peer is a remote station whose radio path contributes to the analysis: a transmitter in RX, or a receiver in TX.

Performance examines the Target on its own. Benchmark compares it with one Reference — another controlled signal path or a known station.

Your question Choose What it examines
What can this station receive, and how consistently? RX Performance Remote transmitters heard by the Target, with activity evidence supporting successes and misses.
Where is this station heard, and how consistently? TX Performance Remote receivers hearing the Target, with activity evidence supporting successes and misses.
How do two receiving setups compare? RX Benchmark Target and Reference receiving the same remote transmitters in the same WSPR cycles.
How do two transmitting setups compare? TX Benchmark The same remote receivers hearing Target and Reference in the same WSPR cycles.

Performance is useful for establishing a station baseline or investigating geographic and time patterns. Benchmark can compare antennas, feedlines, receivers, filters, preamplifiers or complete setups. An independent Reference compares complete installed stations, including their sites and noise environments; it is not automatically a calibrated standard.

0.3 What one run produces

A run processes reports for one analysis choice, band, UTC window and set of settings. It produces maps, summaries over distance and time, station tables and individual observations you can inspect.

The physical experiment is the on-air setup and operation that produced those reports. Changing how you display a completed run does not create a new experiment.

Use the results to ask whether a pattern is broad or limited to a few stations, whether it persists over time, and how much evidence supports it. The results and their settings can be downloaded for later review; your own setup notes complete the record.

0.4 Your first useful run

Start with Load Demo. Choose a maintained example, read its experiment notes and follow the app's setup and run controls, keeping the prepared settings for the first run. Loading settings alone does not start an analysis. In Guided, Walk me through the setup opens the steps; Skip to review and run starts the valid loaded analysis directly.

Follow one feature from the map to its contributing stations and individual observations. Open How to read this beside any result for its metric, legend and interpretation. A demo teaches the method; it is not evidence about your station. Then use Chapter 1 to prepare your own run.
